CONSTANT VELOCITY LAB: Motion Down a Hallway

In this experiment, we will record distance and time data for a toy car and a person as they move down the hallway. We will use everyone in the class to record the distance and time values of the car/person in motion. Each student will have a timer and will be spaced at known intervals along a path. If all students start their timers together and each student stops their own timer when the car passes them, we will have data to draw a distance versus time graph, which when analyzed, will help us to better understand the motion of the objects.

🎯 Goal: To determine and describe the velocity of a toy car and a person moving down a hallway through synchronized time and distance observations.

📐 Set-Up Diagram &amp; Video Guidance

🎥 Watch Setup Video

❓ Pre-Lab Questions &amp; Predictions

Complete these prior to data collection:

Assuming that the car moves at constant speed, draw a motion diagram for the car moving in the positive direction. (Standard notation assumes that right is positive.)
Assuming that the car moves at constant speed, draw a motion diagram for the car moving in the negative direction.
Draw the motion diagram for the person moving in the positive direction at a constant speed that is faster than the car.
We could, using video technology, film the car and create a motion diagram to find the position of the car at known time intervals (Video Example: watch about 15s). In this experiment, we are using stopwatches and meter sticks. How is the data we collect similar to and different from the data collected in a motion diagram?

📋 Method

Each student needs a timer and a way to record times. The class will be positioned along the hallway outside of the classroom spaced every 1 meter.
A teacher or student volunteer will step up to the starting line with the toy car (0 m). On a starting signal, the volunteer lets the car go. Each student should START their timer on the starting signal and STOP their timer when the car passes their position. Concentration is essential so all timers start together!
Record your assigned position and time on paper or a digital device.
Repeat steps 1–3 for the car starting at the opposite end of the hallway.
Repeat steps 1–3 for a person starting at the starting line and moving down the hallway at a constant speed faster than the car.
Return to the classroom to pool and share all data once collection is complete.

📊 Data Presentation

Make three distinct data tables—one for each motion. Title each table and include units for both position (m) and time (s).
Once individual tables are ready, work together as a class to aggregate and share all measured values.

📈 Data Analysis

Using DESMOS or a spreadsheet app, create a graph with position (y-axis) versus time (x-axis) for each recorded motion. You may plot all three on the same graph—just scale your axes so data is well-spread, use distinct colors, and include a descriptive legend. Title your graph and label both axes with units.

Add a Line of Best Fit (LOBF) or trendline for each data set.

Helpful Video Guides Creating Graphs in DESMOS:

▶ Watch Video on DESMOS&nbsp;
Please note: this teachers show how to connect data points on the graph.&nbsp; Please DO NOT do this.&nbsp; Focus on putting data on the graph, scaling, adding color to distinguish different data sets, and adding equations to get Line of best fit. When complete, be sure to screen show your graph and the equations of the lines of best fit.

Questions about the Graphs:

What is the shape of the LOBF that you have drawn on each graph?
What do you learn about the motion of the car/person based on the shape of the LOBF?
How does the shape (and your conclusions about motion) match your observations and predicted motion diagrams?

Calculations:

Using the last data point collected, calculate the average speed of the car/person over the entire interval observed.
Determine the average speed of the car/person from the slope of your line of best fit.

Discussion:

What is similar about the motion of the object (car or human) in all trials?
What is similar about the position graphs?
What is different about the motions/graphs across trials?
Which method of determining average speed (total distance / total time vs. slope of LOBF) is a more accurate representation of speed in this case? Why?
For each motion, did all data points lie on the LOBF? Discuss two potential reasons why or why not.

📌 What to Turn In

In this assignment, you will review key concepts from your summer work. Expressing physical situations through multiple representations—diagrams, words, and graphs—is a core skill for AP Physics 1. Complete the written prompts below to demonstrate your understanding of motion diagrams and graphical analysis.

Written Prompts
Respond to each of the following sections in your submission. Be precise with your physics terminology (e.g., distinguish clearly between distance and displacement, or speed and velocity).

Part 1: Motion Diagrams

Dot Spacing: Explain what equal spacing between dots on a motion diagram indicates about an object's velocity and acceleration. How does this compare to increasing or decreasing spacing?
Vector Representations: Describe how velocity vectors and acceleration vectors are drawn on a motion diagram. How do you determine whether an object is speeding up or slowing down based on the relative directions of v and a?

Part 2: Graphical Representations of Motion

Position vs. Time (x vs. t): Explain what the slope of a position-time graph represents. What does a straight line vs. a curved line signify? What does a negative slope indicate?
Velocity vs. Time (v vs. t): Explain what the slope of a velocity-time graph represents. What does the area under the curve represent? How can you tell if an object is changing direction on a v vs. t graph?
Acceleration vs. Time (a vs. t): Explain what the area under an acceleration-time graph represents. What does a horizontal line at a = 0 mean for an object's motion?

Part 3: Connecting Representations (Synthesis)
Consider a car initially traveling rightward that hits the brakes and comes to a complete stop. Describe how this scenario would look across all four representations:

Motion diagram (dots and vectors)
Position vs. Time graph
Velocity vs. Time graph
Acceleration vs. Time graph

Submission Instructions:
Submit your responses as a Google Doc. You may include hand-drawn sketches or diagrams by taking a clear photo and embedding them directly into your write-up.

In this assignment, you will deepen your understanding of 1D kinematics by connecting mathematical formulas to graphical models. You will review core kinematic equations, analyze instantaneous velocity, and demonstrate how to extract physical quantities like acceleration from curve fits on position graphs. Complete the prompts below using precise physics terminology.

Written Prompts
Respond to each of the following sections in your submission. Show all algebraic steps and explain your reasoning clearly.

Part 1: Review of Kinematic Formulas

Core Equations: State the three primary kinematic equations for 1D motion under constant acceleration. Clearly define each variable (x, x0, v, v0, a, t).
Conditions for Use: What fundamental assumption must be true about acceleration in order to use these kinematic formulas? What happens if this condition is not met?

Part 2: Relationship Between Linear Velocity &amp; Quadratic Position Graphs

Graph Shapes: Explain why a motion with constant, non-zero acceleration produces a linear velocity vs. time graph (v vs. t) but a quadratic/parabolic position vs. time graph (x vs. t).
Slope Connection: Describe how the slope of the quadratic position graph at any instant t1 relates directly to the value on the linear velocity graph at that exact same time.

Part 3: Instantaneous Velocity

Instantaneous vs. Average: Differentiate clearly between average velocity (vavg = Δx / Δt) over a time interval and instantaneous velocity (v) at a single moment.
Tangent Line Analysis: Explain step-by-step how to graphically determine the instantaneous velocity of an object at a specific time t from a curved position vs. time graph.

Part 4: Determining Acceleration from a Quadratic Line of Best Fit (LOBF)

Comparing Fit to Physics: When you fit a quadratic curve to a position vs. time graph (e.g., in DESMOS or Graphical Analysis), the software output takes the form:
x(t) = A·t² + B·t + C
Compare this line-of-best-fit equation directly to the position kinematic formula:
x(t) = xὠ + vὠ·t + ½·a·t²
Extracting Acceleration: Show mathematically how the coefficient A from your quadratic curve fit relates to the object's acceleration a. If your curve fit gives A = 1.8 m/s², what is the physical acceleration of the object?

Submission Instructions:
Submit your responses as a Google Doc. Ensure all algebraic steps, variable definitions, and graph descriptions are written out completely.
